A recent hike in [Königssee](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6nigssee "Königssee on Wikipedia") to see [Germany's highest waterfall](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%B6thbachfall "Röthbach waterfall on Wikipedia") was [a little underwhelming](http://www.alexlomas.com/blog/2012/08/today-we-drove-5km-took-a-boat-12km/ "Europe's highest waterfall on Alex's blog") but the hydrological shortcomings were entirely forgotten when I spotted this beauty growing in a crevice above the lake:

Butterworts are amongst my favourite carnivorous plants: their murderous ways are so delicately concealed. Who would suspect such a pretty bank of flowers to be a veritable morgue of fungus gnats?

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/Pinguicula_alpina.jpg"><img loading="lazy" title="Pinguicula alpina [cc-by-sa-3.0 Steve Cook]" alt="Pinguicula alpina [cc-by-sa-3.0 Steve Cook]: bank of alpine butterworts in flower in Slovenia" src="/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/Pinguicula_alpina-300x225.jpg" /></a><figcaption><em>Pinguicula alpina</em> in Slovenian Alps</figcaption></figure>

I've seen wild butterworts once before (in the Slovenian Alps), where they were obligingly in flower, as you can see above. The two commonest species in the Alps are the white-flowered *Pinguicula alpina* (alpine butterwort) and the purple-flowered *Pinguicula vulgaris *(common butterwort): the latter is also found in the UK, but is by no means 'common' here. The photo at the top could be either of them, but without the giveaway flowers it's difficult to tell which.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/Pinguicula_alpina_flower.jpg"><img loading="lazy" title="Pinguicula alpina flower [cc-by-sa-3.0 Steve Cook]" alt="Pinguicula alpina flower [cc-by-sa-3.0 Steve Cook]: detail of butterwort flower. Flower is white and zygomorphic with five petals, lowermost petal is distinctly hairy and throat of flower leading to nectar tube is yellow" src="/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/Pinguicula_alpina_flower-300x225.jpg" /></a><figcaption><em>Pinguicula alpina</em>, detail of butterwort flower</figcaption></figure>

Like many carnivorous plants using the' flypaper' trapping mechanism, butterworts hold their flowers at some distance from their leaves: the usual explanation for this is that it helps the plant to avoid accidentally capturing its pollinators, but I can find no proper data that support this. The butterworts' pollinators are evidently fairly large: probably butterflies with long tongues, judging by the very long nectar tube, which you can see in the image of one of the other native UK species (*Pinguicula grandiflora*) below:

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/pinguicula_grandiflora_flowers.jpg"><img loading="lazy" title="Pinguicula grandiflora flowers [cc-by-sa-3.0 Alex Lomas]" alt="Pinguicula grandiflora flowers [cc-by-sa-3.0 Alex Lomas]: flowers are purple, each has a nectar tube about 15 mm long projecting from the back of the flower" src="/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/pinguicula_grandiflora_flowers-300x154.jpg" /></a><figcaption>Each <em>Pinguicula grandiflora</em> flower has a nectar tube about 15 mm long projecting from the back of the flower</figcaption></figure>

Butterflies don't strike me as likely to end up as accidental prey, considering the usual prey is extremely small (about 1 mm) flies:

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/Pinguicula_moranensis_drowning_gnat.jpg"><img loading="lazy" title="Pinguicula moranesis drowning a gnat [cc-by-sa-3.0 Steve Cook]" alt="Pinguicula moranesis drowning a gnat [cc-by-sa-3.0 Steve Cook]: fungus gnat trapped in mucilage secreted by stalked glands covering the leaf surface" src="/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/Pinguicula_moranensis_drowning_gnat-300x190.jpg" /></a><figcaption>Death by butterwort</figcaption></figure>

It'd be [pretty easy to design an experiment to investigate this properly](http://rsbl.royalsocietypublishing.org/content/7/2/168 "Blackawton bees, a school science project published by the Royal Society"), perhaps with the cape sundew *Drosera capensis* or Alice sundew  *Drosera aliciae *which are not closely related, but which use a similar trapping mechanism. If you'd be interested, let me know!
