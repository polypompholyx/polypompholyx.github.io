It's nice to find out that - old and jaded as I am - biology can still delight me.

Earlier this week, I covered a first-year practical teaching microscopy skills to first-years, through the medium of pond water. As well as the usual [desmids](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desmidiales "Desmids at Wikipedia"), [rotifers](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotifer "Rotifers at Wikipedia"), [*Daphnia*](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daphnia "Daphnia at Wikipedia") and [nematodes](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nematode "Nematodes at Wikipedia"), one lucky student found a little clump of *[Vorticella](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorticella "Vorticella at Wikipedia") *(or some near relative, I'm not a protistologist!)

Their German name [Glockentierchen](http://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glockentierchen "Glockentierchen on German Wikipedia") ("little bell creatures") delights me almost as much as they do:

Miracle it worked at all!</figcaption></figure>

They're actually quite common in freshwater, but this is the first time I've seen them in our pond samples. Each one is a single cell, attached to the substrate by a thin stalk, which can contract like a spring. Despite their cute bounciness, they're voracious predators: the 'head' end has a cell-sized mouth (cytostome) surrounded by tiny beating hairs (cilia) which help funnel prey into the cell to be digested. The diagram below is of *Stentor* rather than  *Vorticella*, but gives you the rough idea:

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/stentor_diagram.png"><img loading="lazy" title="Stentor diagram [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" alt="Stentor diagram [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" src="/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/stentor_diagram-300x198.png" /></a><figcaption><em>Stentor</em> is another predatory ciliate with a similar form to <em>Vorticella</em>: the main difference being that the 'stalk' in <em>Stentor</em> is much fatter, whereas that of <em>Vorticella</em> is thin and contractile.</figcaption></figure>

*Vorticella* is one example of a very large group of single-celled organisms called [ciliates](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciliate "Ciliates on WIkipedia"), which include the slightly more well known *Paramecium*. Their cells are amazingly intricate, and have sub-cellular compartments (organelles) playing similar roles to those that multicellular organisms have entire organs for. That such complexity can exist in a drop of pond-water smaller than your fingernail is one of the things that makes biology truly awesome.
