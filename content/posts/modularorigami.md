  html: <p>They're both made using the same <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H7qE_Tc8e4g" rel="nofollow ugc">triangle edge module</a> as is used to make the patchwork icosahedron in the same image. When you make the icosahedron, you arrange the 30 modules in groups of fives around each vertex, but for the octahedron, you arrange 12 modules in groups of fours around each vertex - same technique as for an octahedron made of <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_8ftAKXZ2Rc" rel="nofollow ugc">sonobe modules</a>. The tetrahedron requires just 6 triangle edge modules, arranged in threes - it's quite difficult to get all the bits tucked in, but not impossible.</p>

It's much easier to actually make a perfect 3-colour model with the PhiZZ units than with the Sonobe - the Schlegel diagrams shown in the post above re much easier to interpret because  PhiZZ gives you a wire-frame look that's easy to map onto the Schlegel diagram. With Sonobe, you have to try much harder to visualise where the edges actually are. It's definitely do-able, but I would suggest trying a 3-colour 90 PhiZZ first to help get your eye in.</p>

A few months ago, I went to a creative origami lunchtime session organised by some lovely people at `$WORK`. I'd done origami a bit when I was younger, but mostly just frogs and cranes, which have since helped me while away the hours when invigilating exams. However, at this lunchtime session I was shown how to make modular origami. This involves making lots of (generally quite simple) origami parts, and then slotting them together to make larger structures.

[<img src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/sonobe_origami_assembly-300x126.png" class="aligncenter size-medium wp-image-2139" width="300" height="126" alt="Sonobe origami assembly [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" />](/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/sonobe_origami_assembly.png)

I went home with a simple 12-unit Sonobe ball that afternoon and was very pleased with myself.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:285px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/cumulated_octadedron_sonobe.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/cumulated_octadedron_sonobe-285x300.jpg" alt="Cumulated octadedron (sonobe) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Entry-level modular origami: the 12-unit Sonobe ball. Mathematically, it's a cumulated octahedron; practically, it's 12 sheets of square paper and about 1 hour of your time.</figcaption></figure>

 

Things have rather escalated from there.

[<img src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/assorted_modular_origami-300x243.jpeg" class="aligncenter size-medium wp-image-2109" width="300" height="243" alt="Assorted modular origami [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" />](/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/assorted_modular_origami.jpeg)

Between running some of these lunchtime sessions myself now, and being asked on several occasions on Twitter about how I make the pretty things I keep tweeting, I thought it'd be useful to put together a quick guide (or a link-farm, at least)..

[Sonobe units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonobe) are [very easy to fold](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TKGW2W168H0), quite forgiving, and can be used to make a cube (6 units), a [cumulated octahedron (](http://mathworld.wolfram.com/Cumulation.html)12 units), a [cumulated icosahedro](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_gxqwSuvQHU)n (30), and a kind-of [truncated icosahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truncated_icosahedron) (90, basically a spiky football). They're a pretty good introduction to the general principles:

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/assorted_sonobe.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/assorted_sonobe-300x190.jpg" alt="Assorted (sonobe) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Sonobe family: 90, 30, 12, 6 and 3 units. The 3-unit one is a trigonal bipyramid but barely counts! These have all been made with the slightly modified unit mentioned below. The 90 units one is the biggest Sonobe that's really worth making IMHO: about 3 hours' work</figcaption></figure>

The 30-unit ball has the symmetries of an [icosahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_icosahedron) (or [dodecahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_dodecahedron)). Once you've learnt how to construct that object in Sonobe modules, you've essentially learnt how to construct any 30-unit modular origami ball: they mostly involve slotting 30 edge-units into groups of three to form the 12 pentagonal faces of a dodecahedron (or equivalently/alternatively, slotting them into groups of five to form the 20 triangular faces of an icosahedron - the difference is mostly one of perspective).

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/cumulated_icosahedron_sonobe.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/cumulated_icosahedron_sonobe-300x284.jpg" alt="Cumulated icosahedron (sonobe) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Cumulated icosahedron made of Sonobe units: 30 sheets of paper, and - if you've got the hang of the 12-unit ball - still only 1 hour of your time</figcaption></figure>

There are lots of variations on the Sonobe unit you can (re)invent, by adding back-folds that expose the other side of the paper, or that make the tabs narrower than the pockets, giving a more intricate look.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/cumulated_icosahedron_sonobe_variant.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/cumulated_icosahedron_sonobe_variant-300x282.jpg" alt="Cumulated icosahedron (sonobe variant) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Cumulated icosahedron made of slightly modified Sonobe units</figcaption></figure>

Although the 90-unit structure is quite stable, the next one up (270 units) tends to sag under its own weight over time, but by that point it felt like a right of passage to make one.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/270_sonobe_colourchange.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/270_sonobe_colourchange-300x300.jpg" alt="270 (sonobe colourchange) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>9 hours construction, plus some planning. This uses duo paper, which is coloured on both sides, and a modified Sonobe unit that has a reverse fold to expose the other side of the paper in each module.</figcaption></figure>

The Sonobe units can also be assembled inside-out to make inwardly cumulated polyhedra...

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/cumulated_postive_negative_icosahedra_sonobe.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/cumulated_postive_negative_icosahedra_sonobe-300x164.jpg" alt="Cumulated postive negative icosahedra (sonobe) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Getting the last few units in on the inverted ball (left) is tricky.</figcaption></figure>

...and they can also be assembled in pairs and then assembled into a [spiked pentakis dodecahedron.](http://mathcraft.wonderhowto.com/how-to/modular-origami-make-truncated-icosahedron-pentakis-dodecahedron-more-0131528/)..

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/pentakis_dodecahedron_sonobe_colourchange_pairs.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/pentakis_dodecahedron_sonobe_colourchange_pairs-300x294.jpg" alt="Pentakis dodecahedron (sonobe colourchange pairs) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Pentakis dodecahedron, with a reverse-fold Sonobe unit that shows the other side of the paper.</figcaption></figure>

...and other structures.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/rhombic_triacontahedron_sonobe_colourchange_pairs.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/rhombic_triacontahedron_sonobe_colourchange_pairs-300x283.jpg" alt="Rhombic triacontahedron (sonobe colourchange pairs) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>The site above describes this as a rhombic triacontahedron, but I'm pretty sure it isn't. I'm not sure what it actually is though. Has both colour-change and the units are assembled 'inside out' to make it inwardly cumulated.</figcaption></figure>

The next unit I tried was the Penultimate edge unit ([attributed to Robert Neal](http://web.eecs.utk.edu/~plank/plank/pics/origami/penultimate/intro.html)), which can be used to [make a wireframe dodecahedron,](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TV7sbaffuNo&feature=youtu.be) as demonstrated by Matt Parker, the stand-up mathematician. Other variations of this subunit can be used to make pretty much any other wireframe polyhedron.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/dodecahedron_penultimate.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/dodecahedron_penultimate-300x296.jpg" alt="Dodecahedron (penultimate) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Dodecahedron. I was trying to use up the boring coloured paper on this one, but I quite liked the result in the end!</figcaption></figure>

Thomas Hull's [PhiZZ edge unit](http://mattersofgrey.com/phizz-unit-origami-torus/) makes similar wireframe structures, but the modules fit together more tightly and the resulting structures are much more robust than you get with the penultimate modules.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/truncated_icosahedron_phizz.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/truncated_icosahedron_phizz-300x225.jpg" alt="Truncated icosahedron (phizz) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Truncated icosahedron - this is basically the shape of a football (12 pentagons, surrounded by hexagons) and of some viral capsids too.</figcaption></figure>

You can also make colour-change variants using the technique shown in [Lewis Simon's](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vftcf6RKgos) decoration boxes.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:297px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/dodecahedron_phizz_colourchange.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/dodecahedron_phizz_colourchange-297x300.jpg" alt="Dodecahedron (phizz colourchange) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Dodecahedron made from PHiZZ units with a colour-change.</figcaption></figure>

For structures based on dodecahedra/icosahedra and made from edge-units, you can always get away with using just three colours and never have two of the same colour pieces touching. This is because you can draw a [Hamiltonian circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_path) on a dodecahedron: that is a path from vertex to vertex that only visits each vertex once, and which comes back to where it started. You can represent this in 2D on a [Schlegel diagram.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlegel_diagram)

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/hamiltonian_circuit_dodecahedron.png"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/hamiltonian_circuit_dodecahedron-300x290.png" alt="Hamiltonian circuit through a dodecahedron [CC BY-SA 3.0 Tomruen/Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Hamiltonian circuit through the Schlegel diagram of a dodecahedron [CC BY-SA 3.0 original by Tomruen, modified by Steve Cook]. The red and purple edges form the Hamiltonian circuit; the grey edges are what is left over. You'll notice that every vertex has one of each of the three coloured edges. The diagram is a projection of a dodecahedron: imagine taking a wireframe of the dodecahedron and shining a torch through it: the Schlegel diagram is the 2D shadow this 3D polyhedron casts on the wall. It's <em>fairly</em> easy to work out which edge in the 2D diagram correspond to which edge in the thing you're building.</figcaption></figure>

If you colour alternate edges of the Hamiltonian circuit in two of your chosen colours, and the rest of the edges in the third, then you'll avoid having any colour-clashes. I only learnt this after I started making these structures, so not all of them have this optimal colouring! The same 3-colour rule is true for the other Platonic solids, and also for [the truncated icosahedron](http://www.happyfolding.com/gallery-truncated_icosahedron_3_colouring).

[Francesco Mancini's star-holes kusudama](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kJKuyigpb3c) uses a similar module to the PHiZZ, but with a little back-bend that gives a nice 3D star effect. This one is a dodecahedron-shaped (30 units), but a 90-unit truncated icosahedron should also be possible.

UPDATE: yes, it is possible :)

[Lewis Simon and Bennett Arnstein's triangle edge unit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H7qE_Tc8e4g) can be used to make very nice patchtwork tetrahedra, octahedra and icosahedra.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/icosahedron_triangle_edge.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/icosahedron_triangle_edge-300x285.jpg" alt="Icosahedron (triangle edge) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Icosahedron.</figcaption></figure>

They're a bit fiddly to put together but are very robust once constructed. A similar patchwork effect for the dodecahedron can be achieved with M. Mukhopadhyay's [umbrella module](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bA18L8RPor0); Sonobe units can be used to make analogous Battenberg-cake style cubes.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/assorted_triangle_edge_sonobe_umbrella.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/assorted_triangle_edge_sonobe_umbrella-300x177.jpg" alt="Assorted (triangle edge sonobe umbrella) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Battenberg-cake Platonic solids. The dodecahedron is made from umbrella units; the cube from Sonobe. The tetrahedron, octahedron and icosahedron are all made from triangle edge modules.</figcaption></figure>

The [simple isosceles triangle unit](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZbtZU2NCtE) (attributed variously to M. Mukhopadhyay, Jeannine Mosely and Roberto Morassi) can be used to make small and great stellated dodecahedra.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/small_and_great_stellated_dodecahedron_isosceles.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/small_and_great_stellated_dodecahedron_isosceles-300x163.jpg" alt="Small and great stellated dodecahedron (isosceles) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Great (left) and small (right) stellated dodecahedra.</figcaption></figure>

The [small stellated dodocahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_stellated_dodecahedron) is particularly pleasing and makes a fairly robust decoration if made of foil-backed paper.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/small_stellated_dodecahedron_isosceles.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/small_stellated_dodecahedron_isosceles-300x276.jpg" alt="Small stellated dodecahedron (isosceles) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Xmas decs</figcaption></figure>

The great stellated dodecahedron can be made from the same subunit, but is tricker to construct because a tab has to curl around into a pocket that is partly inside the next tab round. I used needle-nosed forceps to construct this, and I'm still not terribly happy with the result.

The opposite is true for [Paolo Bascetta's star module](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R1XTt73diyY), which makes a great great stellated dodecahedron, but a rather \*eh\* small stellation. This module needs duo paper (*i.e.* paper that is coloured on both sides) for best effect.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/small_and_great_stellated_dodecahedron_star.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/small_and_great_stellated_dodecahedron_star-300x138.jpg" alt="Small and great stellated dodecahedron (star) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Great (left) and small (right) stellated dodecahedra.</figcaption></figure>

[Dave Mitchell's Electra module](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M3MEZunaypg) can be used to make a icosidodecahedron: it's unusual in that each module corresponds to one vertex of the structure: the edge units described up to this point combine together to make each vertex.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:295px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/icosidodecahedron_electra.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/icosidodecahedron_electra-295x300.jpg" alt="Icosidodecahedron (electra) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Icosidodecahedron made from Electra modules</figcaption></figure>

I'm not that happy with my [Void kusudama](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XvhVYrAUJf4) (Tadashi Mori): I should have used duo paper, but it was really tricky to put together. Maybe one day. It's one of the few structures here that is back to the original octahedral/cubic 12-unit structure. I'm not sure the 30-unit version would be stable.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/octahedral_void_modified.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/octahedral_void_modified-300x298.jpg" alt="Octahedral void (modified) [CC-BY-SA-4.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Octahedral void (modified)</figcaption></figure>

[Tomoko Fusè's little turtle modules](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qZEoVlAJqcI) are extremely flexible: they can be used to make pretty much any polyhedron that is made of regular polygons. However, because the flaps are only one paper layer thick, they don't fit together terribly tightly, so I've only found them robust enough to make smaller structures without the help of glue. However, with glue, I've made a [rhombicosidodecahedron,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhombicosidodecahedron) which is cool because it is built of pentagons, triangles *and* squares (all of the polygons found in the Platonic solids)...

The [five intersecting tetrahedra](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zU-phqRhQpk) are actually a lot easier to make than they look. [Francis Ow's 6-degree modules](http://mars.wne.edu/~thull/fit.html) themselves are easy to fold, and the vertices are a lot more robust than they might appear. The most difficult bit is getting the modules interlinked in the right way. I've managed it twice, but only whilst staring at the YouTube video and performing assorted "purple = green" gymnastics in my head.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/compound_of_five_tetrahedra.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/compound_of_five_tetrahedra-300x291.jpg" alt="Compound of five tetrahedra [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Compound of five tetrahedra - party piece.</figcaption></figure>

[Michał Kosmulski's page](http://michal.kosmulski.org/origami) has lots of lovely illustrations, instructions and inspirations. I found [Tung Ken Lam's blintz icosadodecahedron](http://mvg-ori.nl/ori-l/models/units/blinicos.pdf) (also credited as [Francesco Mancini's  UVWXYZ intersecting planes model](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zVMBnEHDmGE)) there. It has the same symmetry as the Electra icosadodecahedron above, but you can see the six intersecting pentagons more clearly. Both have the same underlying structure as the [Hoberman sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoberman_sphere) - that expanding/contracting plastic stick model thing beloved of science fairs.
