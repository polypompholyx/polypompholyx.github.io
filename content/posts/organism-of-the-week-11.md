It's not much to look at, but *Psilotum nudum*'s naked fronds* *hold a cautionary tale for biologists:

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:224px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/Psilotum_nudum_Oxford.jpg"><img loading="lazy" alt="Psilotum nudum (Oxford Botanic Gardens) [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" src="/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/Psilotum_nudum_Oxford-224x300.jpg" /></a><figcaption><em>Psilotum nudum</em>, finally bagged at the Oxford Botanic Gardens</figcaption></figure>

*Psilotum* looks a great deal like some of the earliest fossils of land plants. One of the earliest such fossils is *Rhynia*, the first specimens of which which were unearthed in Aberdeenshire around 1910, a mere 410 million years after they were originally buried.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:213px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/Rhynia_reconstruction.jpg"><img loading="lazy" alt="Rhynia gwynne-vaughanii reconstruction [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Griensteidl, based on Kenrick, P &amp; Crane, P. R. (1997) The origin and early diversification of land plants. A cladistic study. Smithsonian Institution Press, Washington and London, fig. 4.8, p. 101]" src="/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/Rhynia_reconstruction-213x300.jpg" /></a><figcaption><em>Rhynia gwynne-vaughanii</em> reconstruction [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Griensteidl]</figcaption></figure>

*Psilotum* and *Rhynia* are anatomically quite similar, despite the latter being 410 million years dead, and the former being very much still alive.

They both have a horizontal stem called a rhizome, from which upright stems grow. These upright stems branch into two equal sub-branches again and again and again in both species. Both plants carry reproductive structures called sporangia (below) on these branches. They both have relatively simple plumbing inside their stems, and they lack true roots and leaves, although both plants appear to be [associated with symbiotic fungi](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycorrhiza "Mycorrhiza at Wikipedia").

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/Psilotum_nudum_sporangia.jpg"><img loading="lazy" alt="Psilotum nudum sporangia [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Eric Guinther (Marshman@Wikipedia]" src="/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/Psilotum_nudum_sporangia-300x225.jpg" /></a><figcaption><em>Psilotum nudum</em> sporangia [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Eric Guinther Marshman@Wikipedia]</figcaption></figure>

As a result of these similarities, *Psilotum* has often been fingered as a primitive relic of a bygone age, and - until someone corrects it - [its Wikipedia entry](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psilotum_nudum "Psilotum nudum at Wikipedia") will continue to claim:  

>   
>
> *Psilotum nudum*, \[...\] is considered a "primitive" plant - a descendent of possibly the first group of vascular plants which were widespread during the Devonian and Silurian periods.
>
>   

  
Interpreting fossils is not at all easy, and this is not a gripe at palaeontologists. However, this tendency to see some organisms as primitive relics, and others (generally the ones we eat, or the ones we *are*) as advanced, is as tempting as it is wrong-headed, and it needs to be resisted. [*All* land-plants are descendents](/2012/04/why-living-fossils-need-to-die/ "Why living fossils need to die") of the first algae that started made a living on land during the Devonian, this is not a singular characteristic of *Psilotum*, or whatever poor organism has been tarred with the primitive brush this week.

In *Psilotum*'s case, not only is dismissing the plant as a primitive relic wrong-headed, it's completely misleading. *Psilotum* is *not* a primitive relic, because the whole concept is nonsense; but it's not *even* a primitive relic on this concept's own nonsensical terms.

Recent molecular data place *Psilotum* not as a ["primitive" or "basal"](/2012/07/magnolia-misunderstanding/ "The magnolia misunderstanding") out-group to the rest of the land-plants, but simply [as an odd-looking fern](http://dx.doi.org/10.1186/1471-2148-13-8 "Grewe, F., Guo, W., Gubbels, E. A., Hansen, A. K. & Mower, J. P. (2013) Complete plastid genomes from Ophioglossum californicum, Psilotum nudum, and Equisetum hyemale reveal an ancestral land plant genome structure and resolve the position of Equisetales among monilophytes. BMC Evolutionary Biology, 13, 8.").

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/fern_phylogeny.png"><img loading="lazy" alt="Fern phylogeny [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook with images from Alex Lomas, Peter Woodard and Abalg]" src="/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/fern_phylogeny-300x144.png" /></a><figcaption>Fern phylogeny [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook with images from Alex Lomas, Peter Woodard and Abalg]. <em>Asplenium</em> and <em>Marattia</em> repreent the two major lineages of ferns-that-look-like-ferns-ought-to, <em>Ophioglossum</em> is an <a title="Ophioglossales at Wikipedia" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophioglossales">adder's tongue fern</a>, <em>Equisetum</em> is a <a title="Equisetopsida at Wikipedia" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equisetopsida">horsetail</a>. This phylogeny differs slightly from the <a title="Vegetable empire 2013" href="/2013/03/vegetable-empire-2013/">Vegetable Empire 2013</a> I posted last week in the placement of the horsetails. Such is the nature of this fast-moving field.</figcaption></figure>

*Psilotum*'s resemblance to fossils like *Rhynia* and *Psilophyton* (below) is illusory. It is a modern organism nested within a group of plants whose most recent common ancestor had leaves and roots and all the other trappings of fern-hood. *Psilotum* is not primitive, because the word is meaningless when applied to species (and to more inclusive taxa). But even if we did accept that some plants are inherently primitive, *Psilotum* doesn't fit the bill: its seemingly primitive leafless and rootless state turns out *not* to be the result of retaining these features from early land plants like *Rhynia*, but of secondarily *losing* leaves and roots possessed by the common ancestor of all ferns.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:164px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/Psilophyton_dawsonii.jpg"><img loading="lazy" alt="Psilophyton dawsonii [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Ghedoghedo; modified by Peter Coxhead]" src="/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/Psilophyton_dawsonii-164x300.jpg" /></a><figcaption><em>Psilophyton dawsonii</em> fossil [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Ghedoghedo; modified by Peter Coxhead]</figcaption></figure>

The cautionary tale of the *Psilotum nudum* is just one example of many face-omelettes that Nature has served up, and will continue to serve up, for as long as *anyone* thinks of *any* organism as the primitive relic of a bygone age.

Can we please let the nonsense word 'primitive' *itself* become a relic of a bygone age?

Pretty please?

With sporangia on top.
