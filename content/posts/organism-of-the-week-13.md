I'm a sucker for things that are [both poisonous and pretty](/2013/01/organism-of-the-week-4/ "Organism of the week #4 – Toadstools"). *Datura metel* definitely meets both criteria.

These photos were taken in southern Spain, where this purple variety of the plant seemed to have naturalised itself out from someone's garden .

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:225px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/Datura_metel.jpg"><img loading="lazy" alt="Datura metel [CC-BY Alex Lomas]" src="/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/Datura_metel-225x300.jpg" /></a><figcaption>Ominously looming over the Spanish countryside</figcaption></figure>

*Datura metel* is closely related to *Atropa belladonna*, the [deadly nightshade](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atropa_belladonna "Deadly nightshade at Wikipedia"), and - like it - the plant contains high concentrations of [atropine](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atropine "Atropine at Wikipedia") and [scopolamine](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scopolamine "Scopolamine"). If eaten, these chemicals clog up receptors in the nervous system, the normal job of which is to cause the body to "[rest and digest](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasympathetic_nervous_system "Parasympathetic nervous system at Wikipedia")". By blocking these receptors, high doses of atropine or scopolamine cause a "flight or fight" response: increased heart rate, dry mouth, reduced gut movement, and dilated pupils. The last of these effects is the source of the *belladonna* ('beautiful lady') of *Atropa belladonna*: juice from deadly nightshade was used cosmetically during the middle ages to dilate the pupils alluringly - and, one suspects - alarmingly.

Atropine and scopolamine are still used medically, but in standardised and pure form rather than as the random rag-bag of assorted poisons used by mediaeval herbalists. Atropine is used to speed up [dangerously slow heart beats](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradycardia "Bradycardia at Wikipedia"), and as a treatment for [organophosphate poisoning](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organophosphate_poisoning "Organophosphate poisoning at Wikipedia"), where it counteracts the excessive "rest and digest" symptoms caused by nerve agents and accidental consumption of some insecticides. Atropine is [no longer used to dilate pupils](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropicamide "Tropicamide at Wikipedia") for eye examinations as it is too long-lasting in its effects (days to weeks!) Scopolamine is mostly used in the treatment of motion sickness: it reduces nausea by reducing motion of the gut.

The fruit of the *Datura* is called a thorn-apple, but I would certainly not recommend baking them into pies. In *Datura stramonium*, the fruit [varies markedly in its shape](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK22088/figure/A474/ "Variation in aneuploid thornapple fruits via NCBI Bookshelf") if it carries an extra copy of one of its chromosomes. Extra chromosomes (up to and including a whole extra set, [or four](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_wheat "Hexaploid wheat at Wikipedia"), or [even eight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fragaria "Decaploid strawberries at Wikipedia")!) are quite well tolerated by plants, although they can lead to fertility issues. However, in humans, most ([but](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Down_syndrome "Down syndrome at Wikipedia") [not](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turner_syndrome "Turner syndrome at Wikipedia") [all](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleinfelter_syndrome "Kleinfelter syndrome at Wikipedia")) changes to chromosome number are lethal. Quite why plants are more tolerant than humans of this sort of large-scale chromosomal change is not well understood. On the other hand, doubling of chromosome number has [occurred at least twice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleopolyploidy "Palaeopolyploidy at Wikipedia") in the evolutionary history of human beings, and our own [chromosome 2](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromosome_2_(human) "Chromosome 2 at Wikipedia") is the product of fusion of two smaller chromosomes (which are retained in our nearest relatives), so these events - whilst rare - are not unheard of in mammals.
