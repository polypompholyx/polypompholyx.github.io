For three centuries, the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death) was routinely epidemic in London. The first outbreak - in 1348 - probably killed half the population of England; [the last outbreak - from 1665 to 1666](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Plague_of_London) - probably killed a quarter of the population of London.

In 1665, Isleworth was a small village several hours' walk (or row) from London proper, but the Great Plague found its way there anyway. As in many places, so many died that digging individual graves became impractical, so instead, the bodies were interred in communal plague pits.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:225px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/Taxus_baccata_plague_pit.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/Taxus_baccata_plague_pit-225x300.jpg" alt="Taxus baccata at All Saints' Isleworth plague pit [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>All Saints' Isleworth plague pit memorial</figcaption></figure>

Isleworth is one of the few places in present-day London where there is evidence above of the burials below. A cairn of stones and a yew tree sit atop the pit and mark the resting place of the 149 people who died there.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/plague_pit_plaque.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/plague_pit_plaque-300x225.jpg" alt="All Saint's Isleworth plague pit plaque [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>"THIS YEW TREE COVERS THE SITE OF THE PLAGUE PIT IN WHICH 149 PERSONS WHO DIED DURING THE GREAT PLAGUE OF 1665 WERE BURIED"</figcaption></figure>

[Yew trees (*Taxus baccata*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxus_baccata) have long had an association with churchyards. The optimistic may consider this appropriate because yews are an evergreen reminder of the life eternal; the cynical may have [other analogies to draw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxus_baccata#Toxicity).

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:225px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/Taxus_baccata_plague_pit_trunk.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/Taxus_baccata_plague_pit_trunk-225x300.jpg" alt="Taxus baccata trunk at All Saints' Isleworth plague pit [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption><em>Taxus baccata</em> yew trunk at All Saints' Isleworth</figcaption></figure>

Since the 1980s, there has been some debate about the cause of the Black Death. The majority of evidence pointed to the [bacterium ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yersinia_pestis)*[Yersinia pestis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yersinia_pestis), *a parasite of rats and other rodents that gets from place to place in the guts of fleas, and causes swelling of lymph nodes, fever, coughing, bleeding under the skin, gangrene and - all too frequently - death.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/micrographia_flea.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/micrographia_flea-300x225.jpg" alt="Flea from Hooke's Micrographia [Public Domain: Steve Cook]" /></a><figcaption>Flea from Robert Hooke's <em>Micrographia</em>. I still can't believe the lovely people at the <a href="http://www.rigb.org/">Royal Institution</a> let me touch it</figcaption></figure>

In particular, an [outbreak of *Y. pestis* plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_plague_pandemic) that started in China in 1885, and continued worldwide until 1959, had similar symptoms to those reported by mediaeval scholars for the Black Death. However, some remained unconvinced, and pointed the finger instead at [haemorrhagic fevers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_hemorrhagic_fever), [anthrax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthrax), or other agents.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/Yersinia_pestis.jpg"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/Yersinia_pestis-300x217.jpg" alt="Yersinia_pestis [Public Domain, credit: NIH]" /></a><figcaption><em>Yersinia pestis</em> bacteria in the gut of a flea [Public Domain, credit: NIH]</figcaption></figure>

[Recent evidence from DNA sequencing](https://dx.doi.org/10.1371%2Fjournal.ppat.1001134) of samples taken from plague pits and other burials appear to back up *Y. pestis *as being the culprit for both the mediaeval Black Death and the even earlier [Justinian Plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Justinian), which devastated the Byzantine Empire in 541-542.

Plague is currently [easily treated with antibiotics like streptomycin](http://www.cdc.gov/plague/healthcare/clinicians.html) if caught early enough, but it's never really gone away: [rodents still carry plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:World_distribution_of_plague_1998.PNG) in the US, India, China, Brazil, and southern Africa, and all of these countries have reported infections in the last 40 years.

It's strange to think that one of the greatest killers in all of human history, exists not just in GCSE history books, but also out there in the real world.

Waiting patiently in the shadows.
