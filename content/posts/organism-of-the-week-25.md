This is another of the things we have found down a microscope in one of our undergraduate practicals, but [for once](/2012/10/glockentierchen/) [it's not](/2013/10/teardrops-of-the-swan/) [a ciliate](/2014/12/stentorian/).

This is the larva of a parasitic fluke called *Bucephalus*, which is the Greek for 'bull headed'. It's appropriate for this fluke not because it looks like [Alexander the Great's horse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucephalus), but because its two "tails" (furcae) look like horns when they are fully extended.

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/haeckel_bucephalus.png"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/haeckel_bucephalus-300x251.png" alt="Haeckel Bucephalus [Public Domain]" /></a><figcaption><em>Bucephalus</em> as interpreted by Ernst Haeckel</figcaption></figure>

Like most of its relatives, *Bucephalus *has a [ludicrously complicated life-cycle](http://dx.doi.org/10.1645/GE-1719.1), making life miserable for no less than three separate hosts: a mollusc (typically a clam), a small foraging fish (like a smelt or goby), and a larger predatory fish (such as a sea-bass):

<figure class="wp-caption aligncenter" style="max-width:300px"><a href="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/bucephalus_lifecycle.png"><img loading="lazy" src="/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/bucephalus_lifecycle-300x187.png" alt="Bucephalus life-cycle [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook, Didier Descouends, Citron, Roberto Pillon]" /></a><figcaption><em>Bucephalus</em> life-cycle [CC-BY-SA-3.0 Steve Cook, Didier Descouends, Citron, Roberto Pillon]</figcaption></figure>

Two of these hosts - the clams and sea-bass - are economically important sea foods. Like many flukes, the larval stages in the first intermediate host - the clam - chemically castrate the host so that it diverts more resources to the survival and propagation of the parasitic fluke than it does to its own. In the sea bass - the definitive host, where the fluke reproduces sexually - the adults are found in the fish's gut, and heavy infections cause weight loss in both wild and farmed fish.

Although *Bucephalus* cannot infect humans, we *are* the definitive host for several other flukes, most importantly the [liver fluke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasciola_hepatica), the [Chinese liver fluke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonorchis_sinensis), the [intestinal fluke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasciolopsis), and the [blood fluke that causes schistosomiasis (bilharzia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schistosomiasis). These have similarly complicated life cycles involving molluscs and assorted other intermediate hosts, and between them they infect many tens of millions of people worldwide.

[Drug treatment options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthelmintic) for fluke diseases tend to be limited and have unpleasant side effects, and schistosomiasis in particular is on the [WHO list of neglected tropical diseases](http://www.who.int/schistosomiasis/en/), for the damning ratio of infections in the developing world to investment made by the developed world in its treatment and prevention. It really is about time this changed.
